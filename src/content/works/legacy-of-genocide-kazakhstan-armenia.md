Genocide has always been personal to me, because it passed through the doors of both my nation and my family. In Kazakhstan, then the Kazakh ASSR, genocide did not occur as a one-time direct overt act of ethnic cleansing; rather, it happened many times in different forms and time periods throughout the twentieth century under the policies of Joseph Stalin within the Soviet Union, remaining officially unrecognized and therefore far more difficult to define and acknowledge within the legal framework of international human rights.

Yet all of these intentional events align with the definition of genocide proposed by Raphael Lemkin, the first person to conceptualize genocide as a global crime. After losing 49 members of his own family in the Holocaust, Lemkin coined the term "genocide" in 1944 by combining the Greek *genos* (race or people) and the Latin *cide* (killing) [1].

In this essay, I argue that the case of Kazakhstan challenges narrow legal definitions of genocide: genocide is not only the physical destruction of people of a particular ethnicity or religion, but also the erosion of their national identity and their future progress. This essay demonstrates how structural and indirect forms of atrocity can profoundly shape a nation's historical and cultural legacy. Through comparison with the Armenian Genocide and the 2023 displacement of Armenians in Artsakh, I examine the similarities in the causes and consequences of mass violence experienced by Kazakh and Armenian populations under imperial rule, asserting that aknowledgement, open dialogue, and the rejection of denialism between governments and affected communities are essential to preventing future genocides.

## Armenian Genocide (1915–1923)

For centuries the great mountain plateau of Eastern Anatolia (in present-day eastern Turkey) was ruled by a succession of Armenian dynasties, although it often faced incursions by outside powers [2]. Armenian political independence was largely brought to an end by a wave of invasions and migrations by Turkic-speaking peoples beginning in the 11th century, and in the 15th and 16th centuries the region was secured by the Ottoman Turks and integrated into the vast Ottoman Empire [2].

The Ottoman Empire was ruled by the Turks who had conquered lands extending across West Asia, North Africa, and Southeast Europe; and its government was centered in Istanbul (then Constantinople) [3]. At that time, the Turks practiced Islam. The 2.5 million Armenians, a Christian minority, started living as second class citizens subject to legal restrictions which denied them normal safeguards: neither their lives, nor their properties were guaranteed security [3].

Between 1912 and 1913, the Empire lost 83 per cent of its European territories during the largely unsuccessful Balkan Wars [4]. This led to an increase in anti-Christian sentiment and amplified the nationalist desire of the Ottoman leaders to create an ethnically homogenous community, hoping that it would then strengthen the empire through shared beliefs and ensure its survival [4]. As the majority of the inhabitants of the Ottoman Empire were Muslim, the Christian Armenians were first increasingly seen as outsiders and a threat to the harmony of the empire [4]. Later, during World War I, when the Turkish army lost at the Battle of Sarikamis in 1915, the Ottoman leaders openly blamed their defeat on Armenian military treachery, concealing the actual failure of poor generalship from the public [5]. Armenian soldiers and other non-Muslims in the army were demobilized and transferred into labour battalions [2]. The disarmed Armernian soldiers were then systematically murdered by Ottoman troops, the first victims of what would become genocide [2].

About the same time, irregular forces began to carry out mass killings in Armenian villages near the Russian border [2]. On April 24, 1915, Talat Paşa (a leader of the Ottoman government) ordered the arrest of approximately 250 Armenian intellectuals and politicians in Istanbul, who were killed in the following months [2]. Throughout summer and autumn of 1915, Armenian civilians were removed from their homes and marched through the valleys and mountains of Eastern Anatolia toward Deir ez-Zor and Ras al-Ayn concentration camps: the deportation was accompanied by a systemic campaign of mass murder carried out by irregular forces as well as by local Kurds and Circassians [2]. Survivors who reached the deserts of Syria languished in concentration camps, many starved to death [2]. Between March and October 1916, there was another wave of executions, and as many as 200,000 more people were murdered [4]. In total, by 1917, at least 664,000 and up to 1.2 million Armenians had been massacred [4]. By 1923 virtually the entire Armenian population of Anatolian Turkey had disappeared [3], yet some Armenians in low-density areas were able to escape execution by converting to Islam (as long as the number of Armenians in the area remained under 5-10% of the total population) [4].

Yepraksia Gevorgyan, a 110-year-old survivor and eyewitness of the Armenian Genocide, tearfully recalled that out of thirty members of her family, only four women (herself, two nieces, and a grandchild) remained by the end of the events. "They killed my father. It was awful to remember, I remember crying all day and night. They were killing people before our eyes: capturing, pillaging, and raping. We were starving to death, begging for bread they would not give us. The water ran the colour of blood: the Turks shot people and they fell into the river during the deportation," recalled Yepraksia Gevorgyan [6].

According to the oral testimony of Lucine Z. Kinoian, a third-generation survivor of the Armenian Genocide, the experiences of her great-grandmother, Anna Tutundijian, also reflect the inhuman suffering endured during the genocide. Anna was 11 years old when she witnessed how all the men, including her father, uncles, and cousins were shot to death [7]. The infant babies were taken from their mothers, including Anna's baby brother: these babies were then buried in the ground only up to their shoulders and horses trampled over them [7]. While evacuating their homes, Anna was kidnapped by a man; and at 13, Anna was already a mother of two: that Turkish abductor was sexually harassing an Armenian child [7].

Young Armenian girls and women were also occasionally spared for forced labour as domestic servants, to become wives in Muslim households or to be used as sex slaves [4].

The current Turkish government does not recognize the Armenian Genocide as genocide, despite the fact that many historians and scholars argue that the events meet the criteria for genocide due to their intentional nature. Although the government acknowledges that deportations occurred, it maintains that Armenians were a rebellious group that had to be suppressed during a time of national security crisis. However, I believe that the Turkish government's refusal to name what happened is not a historical disagreement, it is the final act of the genocide itself, as the denial of memory perpetuates the erasure of an entire people.

As a Kazakh, I read these accounts with a deep understanding of the burden, since these experiences (the deportations, the targeting of intellectuals, the starvation, and the official denial) are not foreign to my own people's history.

## The Kazakh Famine, or Asharshylyk (1930–1933)

From 1930 to 1933, the Soviet Republic of Kazakhstan suffered a horrific yet understudied episode of famine, violence, and displacement that claimed the lives of roughly 1.5 million people [8], which was 40 per cent of the whole population, making it one of the most deadly man-made famines of the twentieth century.

The Kazakh Famine was caused by Stalin's collectivization of agriculture along with a cruel and frankly cynical campaign for the collection of meat, proceeding by way of an absolute exemption of livestock from Kazakh cattlemen by the state [9]. Therefore, in his article for The Yale Review of International Studies, Evan Smith proposes characterizing the Kazakh famine (1930–1933) as a "communist genocide," a term also used by Norman Naimark to encourage scholars to examine the famine through broader interpretations of genocide.

Part of the greater Soviet collectivization famine that also devastated areas of Russia and Ukraine under Stalin's First Five-Year Plan, the Kazakh famine is unique in that it predominantly affected a nomadic society, whose forced sedentarization was a key aim of Soviet policy [8]. Kazakhs, having lost their only source of food (horses, sheep, camel), the population of the villages was doomed to death (90 per cent of Kazakhs were mainly rural at that time) [9].

To avoid famine or requisitions, at least 600,000 people left the republic altogether, using traditional knowledge of seasonal migration routes to seek refuge in Western Siberia, other Central Asian republics, and Xinjiang: thousands of Kazakhs attempting to flee the crisis by traversing the border to Xinjiang were shot by Soviet border guards [8].

Although the Kazakh Famine is not widely recognized as genocide in international law because no recorded evidence has ever been found that Stalin or any other Soviet leader ever expressed a will to wholly or partially eliminate the Kazakh people [8]; however, it can be interpreted as genocide, as it involved the intentional acts of killing of Kazakh ethnicity. For instance, Stalin rejected any international humanitarian help. My paternal great-grandparents' livestock was confiscated and slaughtered during the winter, leaving all of the Kazakh countrymen with nothing. Even lambs were killed, and diseases spread on top of the famine. My grandmother, Sarah, the youngest of eight children born in 1939, survived alongside only one brother; the other six siblings died in the aftermath of Asharshylyk. My grandmother described the famine as so severe that it pushed people to eat dogs, cats, rats, boots, and grass. There were documented cases of cannibalism and mothers giving their fingers to their children to eat: the harshest reality my nation had to survive in.

Open discussion of the famine was severely restricted. No one could speak up for the Kazakh nation, as 66 thousand of the representatives of literate and sagacious Kazakh intellectuals suffered from oppression in 1937–1938 [10]. I believe these repressions can also be understood not only as the targeting of individuals who aspired to establish an independent Kazakh state, but also as a genocide of the nation's intellectual and cultural legacy. The Kazakh intelligentsia, the most educated segment of Soviet Kazakhstan, sought to empower their people through education and reform. However, Stalin tortured and killed them all, ensuring that potential political resistance and alternative national leadership were eliminated and Soviet control over the region was maintained.

As a result, nowadays, a significant portion of ethnic Kazakhs (particularly urban, educated ones) are more comfortable in Russian than in Kazakh. This is a direct first consequence of the destruction of the Kazakh intellectual class and the Russification that followed. The people who would have developed Kazakh as a modern intellectual language capable of expressing philosophy, science, law, and literature were killed. Secondly, there is a huge education gap: the Kazakh intelligentsia were building universities, schools, and curricula focused on Kazakh history in the 1920s. Thirdly, there is no Kazakh intellectual diaspora that kept the flame alive till these days.

Denial does not end genocide; it extends its impact across generations, making remembrance and open discussion critically important in order to prevent the recurrence of such atrocities, as seen in the ethnic cleansing of Armenians in Artsakh on October 10, 2023. Most of the Armenian population from Artsakh, over 100,000, 25% of whom were children, were forcibly displaced into Armenia after nearly 300 days of being deprived of life-saving necessities such as food, medicines and fuel, resulting in starvation and death [11].

Kazakhstan and Armenia could draw lessons from reconciliation models such as the agreement between Germany and Namibia, where Germany formally acknowledged its historical responsibility for the genocide of the Herero and Nama peoples and committed €1.1 billion in development aid over 30 years as part of a broader process of historical reconciliation.

For me, the legacy of genocide means a deeply personal, inherited wound that spans generations, from my grandmother's survival to my people's silenced language, demanding attention and solidarity to prevent history from repeating itself.
