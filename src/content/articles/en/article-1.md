Today’s character of Qazaq Mura journal is Bakytgul. A woman who had to go through an oncological disease that became one of the consequences of exposure to the Semipalatinsk Test Site. Despite pain, fear, and numerous hardships, she managed to put herself back together piece by piece, demonstrate incredible resilience, and overcome the illness. Her story is one of courage, willpower, and boundless love for life. Today we will introduce you to her inspiring life journey.

The Semipalatinsk Nuclear Test Site was the first and one of the largest nuclear test sites of the USSR, located on the territory of present-day Kazakhstan (within the boundaries of the Abai, Pavlodar, and East Kazakhstan regions). Established in 1949, it became the site of the first Soviet atomic bomb test (1949) and hydrogen weapon tests (1953).

The test site covered an enormous area of 18,500 square kilometers (approximately equal to the territory of a whole country such as Slovenia). Over more than 40 years, around 456 nuclear and thermonuclear tests were conducted there, including both atmospheric and underground explosions.

None of the local residents were aware of these tests and were not warned about them. More than 1.5 million people were affected by the Semipalatinsk Test Site. Over 40 years, hundreds of thousands of residents of Kazakhstan were exposed to radioactive contamination, and their descendants are still facing severe genetic and environmental consequences.

## About herself

@Interviewer {
    Bakhytgul, tell us a little about yourself (your age, place of origin, family, professional career, hobbies).
}

@Bakytgul {
    I am 60 years old. I was born in the Semipalatinsk region, in the city of Ayagoz. I grew up in a very large, loving family. My father was a war veteran, a highly respected man who raised fourteen children.
    By profession, I am a teacher. For a long time, and especially in recent years, I worked as a professional trainer and coach. I was fortunate to study under Cambridge professors and learn new approaches and updates in the content of education in Kazakhstan. I am an accomplished professional, holding several international certificates.
    As for my hobbies, I love traveling very much. I was an active driver for many years and felt confident behind the wheel. I also deeply love people and endlessly love life!
}

@Interviewer {
    What does “your native land” mean to you?
}

@Bakytgul {
    For me, my native land is not just a geographical location; it is something deeply sacred. Our land is unique, full of wonders, great history, and immense spiritual strength. Yes, its history has been attempted to be rewritten, but genetic memory cannot be erased.
    In my life, I had several real opportunities to emigrate to other countries and build a comfortable, stable life outside of Kazakhstan. But each time I made a decision, I understood: I am needed here. My roots are here, and I must serve my land.
}

## Illness and diagnosis

@Interviewer {
    On the Semipalatinsk Test Site, there were about 456 nuclear tests conducted over 40 years, approximately 11 explosions per year. Were you and the local residents ever warned that an explosion was about to happen, or did it occur unexpectedly?
}

@Bakytgul {
    We were never, ever warned. Absolutely not. All these tests were shrouded in the strictest secrecy. The residents of the region lived in complete ignorance. We began to learn about what had truly happened, about the terrible force that was destroying our health from within, only years later, after the test site was closed and fragments of information started to emerge. But even today, we most likely still do not know the full truth.
    At that time, it was perceived as something ordinary, hidden under the mask of normal life. But the land around us was contaminated with anomalous radiation stretching for thousands of kilometers. And this radiation took its toll: a massive number of cancer cases, an unexplained surge in suicides among an extremely young population, and the birth of children with severe pathologies and mutations. People were dying without understanding the real cause, believing that it was simply “their fate.”
}

@Interviewer {
    Regarding your body at that time: what unusual sensations did you begin to experience (headaches, strange taste in your mouth, fatigue)? What health problems began to appear in you or your loved ones?
}

@Bakytgul {
    The first thing I clearly remember as a sign of illness was a piercing, indescribable cold inside my body. It could not be explained or warmed away; it came from deep within. Alongside it came overwhelming chronic fatigue and an unexplained persistent fever. This lasted for several months. I was literally fading away before my eyes, losing weight at a catastrophic rate.
    The turning point came one morning when a firm lymph node the size of a pigeon’s egg appeared on my neck, just above the collarbone. My parents became alarmed. I was only 21 years old at the time.
    By a twist of fate, my sister and brother-in-law happened to visit from Almaty during that period. They saw my condition and urgently took me to the capital. That saved my life. I was admitted to the Scientific Research Institute of Oncology and Radiology, into what was known as an experimental ward.
}

@Interviewer {
    At what age were you diagnosed with cancer?
}

@Bakytgul {
    I was 21 years old. The very peak of youth, when life is just beginning. The diagnosis sounded like a death sentence: lymphogranulomatosis (a cancer of the lymphatic system). At that time in Kazakhstan, it was not very common, and our ward was experimental. The doctors were just beginning to learn how to fight this form of cancer, studying us closely as they treated us.
}

@Interviewer {
    Please tell us about the moment when the doctors told you your diagnosis. Where were you, and what did you feel inside?
}

@Bakytgul {
    I spent long, uninterrupted six months within the walls of the institute. Half a year in four walls. The treatment was aggressive; courses of radiation therapy alternated with heavy chemotherapy. Chemotherapy literally burned and poisoned me from the inside, while radiation burned everything indiscriminately, both diseased and healthy cells.
    But you know what is surprising? Despite the severity of the situation, I had absolutely no fear of death inside me. My physical indicators were critical, my tests were close to zero, my leukocytes were “inflated,” and my medical records indicated that I should pass away. The doctors openly told my relatives upon discharge: “She has at most two to three months left. Take her home.” However, inside me lived a quiet, unbreakable certainty: I will live. I simply knew it, in spite of all predictions.
}

## Family and society

@Interviewer {
    How did your family and neighbors react to your cancer diagnosis? Were you alone with this diagnosis, or were there others around you with similar stories?
}

@Bakytgul {
    For my family, it was a terrible ordeal. To stay in an oncology clinic for six months without leaving, watching your child fade away – this is an enormous psychological burden for loved ones.
    As for the environment, neighbors… At that time, the word “oncology” was an absolute synonym for the word “death.” Because of this fear, people reacted differently. Some sincerely sympathized, while others kept their distance. In a way, you became an outcast, people feared cancer as if it could be transmitted through a glance or conversation.
    And inside the hospital itself, death was a daily visitor. People were dying in large numbers. We would go to sleep, and by morning there was movement in the corridors: someone was being taken away, someone was screaming from pain in neighboring wards. I saw people passing away every day. But I repeat: my inner core did not allow me to break; there was no fear.
}

@Interviewer {
    Did the doctors speak to you about a connection with the test site, or was this silenced? How did you come to this understanding yourself?
}

@Bakytgul {
    The doctors in the clinic were silent. In Soviet times, there was a strict taboo on linking diagnoses with nuclear testing. But the understanding came later, when the test site was closed and the scale of the catastrophe became evident.
    When you analyze the number of people who became ill specifically from our region, the nature of mutations, and the types of blood and lymph cancers, everything becomes clear without words. Today I openly call things by their name: what was done to our region was a hidden genocide against my native Kazakh people.
}

## A message to the test site

@Interviewer {
    If the Semipalatinsk Test Site could hear you, what would you say to it?
}

@Bakytgul {
    I would ask: “Why?” I am an adult and I understand the laws of geopolitics. The Cold War was underway, there was the threat of nuclear confrontation, and the country needed to strengthen its defense and maintain parity. But why was this particular region chosen for the test site? A land where people had lived for centuries? Around the test site there were not only small villages; there were large, developed cities with huge populations. Was it really impossible, on the map of a vast state, to find an uninhabited, deserted area in order to minimize human losses?
    I would say to the test site, or rather to those behind it: great powers must be able to negotiate. Any global political ambitions must be resolved at the negotiating table, in a humane and diplomatic way. Human lives are too high a price for nuclear status. A consensus can always be found if the priority is the human being, not the bomb.
}

@Interviewer {
    Do you share this story with your children and grandchildren? Or do you want to protect them from this pain?
}

@Bakytgul {
    My children and relatives, of course, know this story. For them, my life is a tangible, living miracle. Knowing that I was discharged home to die with a prognosis of two months, they look at me and understand what strength a human being can hold.
    Of course, as a mother and grandmother, I pray to God for one thing: that this burden passes over my descendants. I want to believe that this illness remained in the past, that it left and closed itself within me, without leaving a trace in the genetic code of my children and grandchildren. May they be protected.
}

@Interviewer {
    What does it mean to you that we are recording your story today? Is it important for you that Kazakhstan remembers?
}

@Bakytgul {
    It is vitally important. We must remember what a massive, planetary tragedy our people went through.
    But along with memory, I also carry a deep sense of injustice.
    If we look at Japan’s experience (Hiroshima and Nagasaki), we see how, over decades, the state has carefully and extensively supported those affected by radiation. Huge funds are allocated, social and medical programs have been created, and people are surrounded with care until the end of their lives.
    And what did we receive in Kazakhstan? I was paid a single, very small compensation once. No real social support, no specialized medical assistance, no healthcare benefits. Now, as I retire, the entire “gratitude” of the state for the health destroyed by the test site is expressed only in adding six months to my work experience. That is all. I consider this inhumane and deeply unfair toward a generation that became a living shield of that era. That is why speaking about it is our duty.
}

## Source of strength

@Interviewer {
    You survived what would have broken many. Where does your strength come from?
}

@Bakytgul {
    My strength is woven from two things: the strength of a mother’s prayer and the strength of great ancestors.
    I clearly remember one episode. When I was brought home from the hospital “to die,” I accidentally overheard my mother. She was a completely secular woman, not religious, raised in the Soviet era. But I saw her standing on her knees, with tears in her eyes, earnestly praying to God. She said: “God, I have already lived in this world. Take my life, but leave my child alive.”
    These words pierced me. At that very moment, an iron will was born inside me: “I will live. And my mother will live a long life too.” The power of this maternal intention and my own determination created a miracle. My mother lived a long life and passed away at the age of 85, thank God, and this illness never touched her. And I am alive and healthy to this day, despite being declared disabled at 21, which was psychologically very difficult for a young girl.
    As Bulat Okudzhava sings: “We will be saved by a mother’s prayer.” The prayers of my loved ones were heard by God. And, of course, I was incredibly fortunate with my doctor. In our experimental ward, the attending physician was the great specialist Shakar Zhartyovna Balgozhina. She had just returned to her historical homeland, Kazakhstan. Before that, she had been the personal doctor of the famous Olympic champion Lyudmila Pakhomova (who had the same diagnosis but sadly passed away quickly). The fact that I ended up in the hands of such a strong doctor also strengthened my faith. The power of intention and the power of professionalism defeated death.
}

@Interviewer {
    If, 50 years from now, a young Kazakh reads your interview, what is the most important thing you want them to understand?
}

@Bakytgul {
    I want a young Kazakh man or woman reading these lines in 50 years to remember their genetic code.
    I want them to understand that we are a great nation. The blood of people of incredible courage, powerful spirit, and immense inner strength flows in our veins. We endured what others would not have survived.
    The main message I want to pass on to the next generation is this: never, under any circumstances, give up. Whatever happens in life, whatever trials come your way, endure them! Have a firm and clear intention to be worthy sons and daughters of your land, to serve your people, and to honor the memory of your ancestors who preserved and passed on this great, sacred Kazakhstan.
}

## Epilogue

Forty years have passed, but the test site has not left my life completely. It has left deep physiological imprints on my body, with which I have learned to coexist every day, walking this path with dignity and inner calm.

The most severe consequence that has fundamentally changed my daily life is the complete atrophy of sweat glands. My body does not sweat at all; it does not cool itself under external conditions, not at all. Physiologically, I am completely deprived of natural thermoregulation. In hot weather or in a stuffy, unventilated room, when a normal human body cools itself automatically, I must rely on external means. My constant companions for 40 years have been a fan and water spray bottles. I am forced to cool myself manually, imitating what nature should do on its own.

In addition, the circulatory system was severely affected. Despite following a strict hydration regimen, studying and applying proper health approaches, and drinking large amounts of clean water, my blood remains abnormally thick.

Recently, I took medical tests that confirmed what I have intuitively and physically felt for 40 years: my body lives in a state of chronic brain hypoxia. Due to circulatory disorders, tissues and cells do not receive sufficient oxygen. That is why I continue to feel weakness and tire very quickly. To stay functional, I must exert far more effort than an average person. Despite this constant exhaustion, I have always led and continue to lead an active life, working intensely. But behind this activity lies daily endurance.

Analyzing my condition as an expert, I understand that the roots of this hypoxia also lie in deep disruption of cellular metabolism: due to radiation exposure and chemical intoxication, my body does not fully absorb essential micronutrients. Take iron, for example, a key element for oxygen transport. My body simply cannot properly absorb and synthesize it.

And there is another striking marker I notice: in hot weather, when I take a shower, water flows unevenly over my body. The areas that underwent radiation therapy seem to repel water; it runs off them differently. My skin remembers every radiation session.

From a clinical medical perspective, harsh gamma radiation and aggressive chemotherapy 40 years ago did not just destroy the tumor; they burned out sweat gland cells at a deep level, triggered radiation fibrosis, altered bone marrow structure, and permanently damaged vascular endothelium. My cells in those areas have never fully recovered. Hence the thick blood, micronutrient malabsorption, and consequently constant oxygen deficiency in brain tissues. With age, maintaining this balance becomes increasingly difficult, and I must fight every day for my physical condition.

The fact that I not only survived experimental protocols of the 1980s but also maintained a high quality of life, activity, mental clarity, and love for the world at the age of 60 is a true phenomenon. My body did not return to its original “factory state”; the test site permanently reshaped it. But the fact that I live, create, and govern this body confirms my main truth: any illness can be overcome at the level of spirit and neurobiology through iron will and intention.

I want young researchers reading these lines to understand: the tragedy of Semipalatinsk is not just a paragraph in a history textbook or a distant past. It is something that is still, right now, circulating in our blood, taking our strength, reflecting on our skin, and demanding from us daily, invisible courage.
