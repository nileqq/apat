Our featured guest today is Karipbek Kuyukov, a world-renowned Kazakh artist and activist for the international anti-nuclear movement. Born without arms due to the devastating consequences of the Semipalatinsk nuclear test site, he creates his powerful canvases using his mouth and toes, transforming his personal story into a universal symbol of the struggle for a world free of nuclear weapons.

In this interview, Karipbek Kuyukov opens up about his childhood and his journey as an artist, reveals the ultimate goals of his international missions, and delivers a profound message to the future generations of Kazakhstan.

Karipbek Kuyukov was born in 1968 in the village of Egindybulak, located in the Karaganda region (bordering the Abai region, near the former Semipalatinsk province). Today, the master lives and creates his art in Karaganda.

His paintings are held in both private and state collections across the USA, Japan, and Germany. He has successfully exhibited his work in Japan, the United States, Germany, Turkey, China, and Kazakhstan. The central theme of his art revolves around the nuclear threat to humanity and environmental protection.

Since 1989, he has been an active member of the international anti-nuclear movement "Nevada–Semipalatinsk." In 2013, Karipbek Kuyukov addressed the United Nations General Assembly in New York, speaking on the tragedy of the Semipalatinsk test site and calling for global nuclear disarmament.

In 2016, during the Nuclear Security Summit in Washington, D.C., the President of Kazakhstan presented Karipbek Kuyukov’s painting “Explosion” as an official gift to US President Barack Obama.

## Childhood

@Interviewer {
    You were born in the village of Yegindybulak, just 100 kilometers from the Semipalatinsk Nuclear Test Site. What does this land mean to you today?
}

@Karipbek {
    The land where I was born is, without question, the most precious place in my life. But it pains me deeply to think about everything my homeland endured during the forty years of nuclear testing. Today, it is heartbreaking to see what has become of my native village. There was a time when 35,000 people lived here; now, barely 1,500 remain. It breaks my heart to see people leaving their homes and moving to the cities. I also wish the government would do more to support rural communities and create opportunities that would encourage people to stay.
}

@Interviewer {
    What was your childhood like? Where did you grow up?
}

@Karipbek {
    My journey to first grade began in Russia. Although I spent my early years in Yegindybulak, I later studied in Leningrad. My father desperately wanted me to have prosthetic arms so that I could study alongside other children and receive a good education.
    At the time, a research institute in Leningrad was developing advanced prosthetic technology. When its professors offered to create state-of-the-art prosthetic limbs for me, my father was overjoyed. Because the distance between Leningrad and Yegindybulak was so great, I could not travel home often, so I lived in a boarding school attached to the institute. Outside of my classes, I took part in testing newly developed prosthetic devices. Since children grow quickly, they had to be replaced and adjusted every two years.
    In the end, I graduated from school in Russia. I remain deeply grateful to the teachers and mentors who worked with me. They recognized and nurtured my artistic talent, which is what allows me to express myself through painting today.
}

@Interviewer {
    Did you ever experience bullying or discrimination at school because of your disability?
}

@Karipbek {
    I never experienced bullying because I spent most of my school years in a specialized school alongside children with disabilities similar to my own. For about three years, however, I attended a regular public school with able-bodied students. Even there, I never felt that anyone treated me with prejudice or pity because of my disability. I wasn’t a straight-A student, and I enjoyed getting into a little mischief from time to time. In many ways, I had a perfectly ordinary childhood.
}

## The connection to the test site

@Interviewer {
    When did you learn that the reason you were born without arms was the nuclear testing that had taken place long before you were born?
}

> “Semipalatinsk Nuclear Test Site (1949-1991): Over the course of more than 40 years, the Soviet Union conducted more than 450 nuclear tests at the Semipalatinsk Nuclear Test Site, exposing over 1.5 million people to radiation and leaving lasting humanitarian, environmental, and health consequences across eastern Kazakhstan.”

@Karipbek {
    I first learned the truth in 1989, when our poet, Olzhas Suleimenov, spoke openly about it for the first time. Olzhas Omaruly Suleimenov – a world-renowned Kazakh poet, writer, linguist, and public figure – was the first to tell the world what had truly happened at the Semipalatinsk Nuclear Test Site and the devastating harm it had caused to the people living nearby.
    The test site affected the territories of three regions: Karaganda, Pavlodar, and Semipalatinsk. For more than forty years, increasingly powerful nuclear weapons were tested there, releasing enormous amounts of radiation into the environment.
    When the international anti-nuclear movement Nevada-Semipalatinsk was founded, I could not remain on the sidelines. I became one of its active participants.
}

> “Nevada-Semipalatinsk (later Nevada-Semey) was an international anti-nuclear movement founded on February 28, 1989, by renowned Kazakh poet and public figure Olzhas Suleimenov. Its name symbolized solidarity between victims and anti-nuclear activists in Kazakhstan and the United States, linking the Semipalatinsk Nuclear Test Site with the Nevada Test Site. The movement united millions of people in opposition to nuclear testing and played a pivotal role in the closure of the Semipalatinsk Nuclear Test Site in 1991, while bringing global attention to the humanitarian consequences of nuclear weapons.”

@Karipbek {
    We began collecting testimonies from eyewitnesses, people who had witnessed the tragedy with their own eyes. I also have my own memories. As a child, my father often took me on trips, and we would drive past the test site. Scattered across the steppe stood abandoned military equipment: aircraft, tanks, and other machinery used during the nuclear tests.
    After seeing how deeply these tests had affected people’s lives, we could no longer remain silent. It is the duty of every conscientious person to tell the world what happened so that such a tragedy is never repeated. I was not the only victim. Thousands of people suffered. Children’s lives were forever changed. Infant mortality was terrifying: many newborns died within their first days of life.
    For decades, all of this remained classified as a state secret. After the United States dropped the atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki, the Soviet Union was determined to prove that it could match America’s nuclear capabilities. Meanwhile, ordinary people continued living in and around the test site. They worked, raised livestock, and built their lives while radiation silently destroyed their health. People began losing their hair, cancer rates soared, and countless lives were cut short. Yet they were told that this was necessary, that it was the price they had to pay for the security of the nation.
}

## The artist's path

@Interviewer {
    Do you remember the first time you held a paintbrush in your mouth or between your toes?
}

@Karipbek {
    Of course I do. It happened when I was at school. Since I had no arms, my feet became my hands. I had to learn how to adapt to everyday life. I would hold a pencil between my toes or clench it between my teeth and begin drawing. At first, they were just simple children’s doodles in my notebooks, but little by little, I improved.
    I loved spending time in the library and reading constantly. More than anything, I wanted to bring the characters from my favorite books to life on paper. That was how my first drawings were born.
}

@Interviewer {
    Why did painting become your way of speaking to the world?
}

@Karipbek {
    I believe every one of my paintings is a cry from the soul. I have always wanted people not simply to admire my work, but to see and feel the issues behind it. Many of my paintings are devoted not only to the tragedy of nuclear testing, but also to environmental destruction more broadly.
    Whenever something in this world causes me pain or concern, I feel compelled to express it through my art. I realized that painting is the language I was meant to use, a way to communicate with humanity and open people’s eyes to the issues that truly matter.
}

@Interviewer {
    How did you learn the technique of painting? Who taught you, and how long did it take to master it?
}

@Karipbek {
    When I was at school, my teachers encouraged me to join an art club. They believed in me and constantly told me, “You can do it. Keep going. Keep developing your talent.”
    I never received a formal art education. I am entirely self-taught.
    To improve my technique, I spent countless hours in libraries studying books on painting and learning how to mix colors properly. I began with watercolor and acrylic before eventually moving on to oil paints. Oil painting is a warm and expressive medium, but it also comes with many technical challenges that I had to overcome on my own.
    How long did it take? I would say a lifetime. Learning never ends. With every new canvas, I improved a little more, because true mastery comes only through constant practice, perseverance, and patience.
}

@Interviewer {
    How does a painting begin for you? Do you see the image before you start painting, or does it emerge during the process?
}

@Karipbek {
    Every painting begins with a clear vision. Long before I touch the canvas, I already know exactly what I want to say to the world.
    One of the themes that constantly occupies my mind is the tragedy of children in war. In every armed conflict, children are the first to suffer, becoming innocent victims of the ambitions of adults.
    Through my paintings, I try to show that weapons and children can never coexist. Before starting any conflict, we must stop and ask ourselves what kind of future we are leaving for our children and what sort of world they will inherit.
}

@Interviewer {
    Do you think art can convey the horror of nuclear weapons better than statistics or historical facts?
}

@Karipbek {
    Absolutely. Statistics are just numbers on a page, and historical facts can often feel distant. Art, on the other hand, has the power to awaken genuine emotion. Whether it is a powerful film, a song, a theatrical performance, a photograph, or a painting, art leaves a lasting impression on the human soul.
}

@Interviewer {
    Is there one painting that marked a turning point in your artistic journey?
}

@Karipbek {
    Yes. It was my first large-scale painting on the subject of nuclear tragedy, titled “The First Explosion.” Today it is kept in a museum, but at the time it completely changed my life. It was after creating that painting that I truly realized the power of art and began organizing charity exhibitions.
    Every donation raised through the sale of my paintings and exhibitions went to help seriously ill children from my native Yegindybulak district. At that time, there were around 250 families raising children with severe illnesses caused by radiation exposure. With all my heart, I wanted to do everything I could to make their lives a little easier.
    That painting transformed me from simply an artist into a civic activist.
}

## Traveling the world

@Interviewer {
    You have met mothers from Hiroshima and Nagasaki, participated in anti-nuclear protests in Nevada, and spoken with survivors of the Chernobyl disaster. How did these encounters change your understanding of nuclear catastrophe?
}

@Karipbek {
    During my travels, I visited the United States and stood near the Nevada Test Site. There, we joined local organizations in the fight against nuclear weapons because the people living there had suffered just as we had in Kazakhstan. I also met the hibakusha in Japan (the survivors of the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki) as well as people affected by the Chernobyl disaster.
    These encounters taught me that nuclear tragedy has no nationality.
    I will never forget standing shoulder to shoulder with women whose eyes were filled with tears as we protested together, demanding the closure of nuclear test sites. Looking at them, I realized that we all shared the same immense grief.
    It is these people, their courage and their pain, that continue to give me the strength to never give up and to keep fighting for a world free of nuclear weapons.
}

@Interviewer {
    In 1989, you joined the Nevada-Semipalatinsk delegation and traveled to the United States, Germany, Japan, and Turkey. Which moment from those journeys has stayed with you the most?
}

@Karipbek {
    Every trip was meaningful, but the one I remember most vividly was our first visit to Hiroshima and Nagasaki in 1990.
    It was August 6, the day commemorating the victims of the atomic bombing. The weather was unbearably hot. We spoke at the Hiroshima Peace Memorial Park, where around 5,000 people sat before us, listening to every word.
    Olzhas Suleimenov spoke first. He raised his hand, showed his open palm to the crowd, and said: “We have come to you with an open hand and peaceful intentions. We are victims just like you, and our shared goal is to close every nuclear test site on Earth.”
}

> At that time, the world’s five principal nuclear test sites were Semipalatinsk (Kazakhstan), Nevada Test Site (United States), Novaya Zemlya (Russia), Bikini and Enewetak Atolls (Marshall Islands), and Mururoa Atoll (French Polynesia).

@Karipbek {
    When it was my turn to speak, I looked out at the crowd and said: “I cannot make this gesture myself because I have no hands. But I ask you, please raise your open palms on my behalf.”
    At that very moment, all 5,000 people rose to their feet and lifted their open hands into the air. At the same time, the song “Zaman-ai,” performed by Roza Rymbayeva, began to play – our unofficial anthem of the anti-nuclear movement.
    It is impossible to describe that moment. Tears filled all of our eyes. We were overwhelmed with an extraordinary sense of unity, pride, and hope.
    A little later, Olzhas Suleimenov turned to me and quietly said: “Karipbek, this is your mission. From this day forward, you are our messenger of peace.”
}

@Interviewer {
    You have spoken in many countries around the world, including at a United Nations session in New York. What is the most important message you hope to convey to humanity?
}

@Karipbek {
    Speaking from the United Nations, the world’s foremost international platform, was an immense honor and an opportunity to address the global community on behalf of Kazakhstan. My message was directed to the leaders of every nation.
    First, I urged them to recognize that the enormous financial resources currently spent on developing and modernizing weapons of mass destruction should instead be invested in saving lives: in healthcare, environmental protection, and education.
    Second, I called on all nuclear-armed states to sign the Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty (CTBT) and follow Kazakhstan’s example. When Kazakhstan gained its independence, its very first historic decision was to voluntarily renounce the world’s fourth-largest nuclear arsenal. Our country demonstrated extraordinary courage and wisdom. I want world leaders to reflect on one simple truth: if Kazakhstan could make that choice, then the security of our planet must be more important than any nation’s political ambitions.
}

@Interviewer {
    How did you feel when you learned that, in 2016, President Nursultan Nazarbayev presented your painting Explosion to President Barack Obama during the Nuclear Security Summit?
}

@Karipbek {
    I had no idea it was going to happen. I only learned about it afterward from the Embassy of Kazakhstan in the United States.
    It was an incredible honor. I remember thinking that regardless of who occupies the White House, my painting would remain there as a silent reminder of the price Kazakhstan paid and of our country’s unwavering commitment to peace.
    But when I look at how rapidly geopolitical tensions are escalating around the world today, I realize that our past achievements are no longer enough. The nuclear threat is becoming real once again. We have reached a moment when activists, and humanity as a whole, must do more than simply speak. We must raise our voices and act before it is too late.
}

## Forgiveness and strength

@Interviewer {
    Do you feel anger or resentment about the past?
}

@Karipbek {
    No. I do not carry anger or resentment in my heart.
    People often ask me why I have never filed official claims or demanded personal compensation from the government. My perspective is broader than that. The nuclear tests were conducted by the Soviet Union. At that time, we were all citizens of one country. Today, the issue is not about money, it is about basic human justice.
    At the very least, there should be an official apology to the people of Kazakhstan for the immense suffering caused by radiation.
    History offers an important example. After the Second World War, Germany acknowledged the crimes of its past, accepted responsibility, and formally apologized to the nations that had suffered. Even if it comes decades later, I believe a similar acknowledgment from the successor state of the Soviet Union is essential. It is a matter of respect, for those who lost their lives, and for those who continue to live with the pain.
}

@Interviewer {
    Do you believe forgiveness is possible?
}

@Karipbek {
    Yes, because without forgiveness, it is impossible to move forward. To build, to grow, and to live in peace and friendship, we must learn to let go of past grievances, no matter how painful they may be. That is what matters most.
    History has shown us that wars and blind hatred between nations never lead to anything good: they only bring more destruction. Forgiveness is not a sign of weakness. It is the only way to preserve our humanity and give the future a chance.
}

@Interviewer {
    How do you define the word “strength”?
}

@Karipbek {
    Strength is a broad and multifaceted concept. There are different kinds of strength.
    There is destructive strength – the kind that brings humanity nothing but pain, suffering, devastation, and grief. We see it in weapons and in war.
    But there is another kind of strength: strength of spirit. It is the ability to remain human despite any hardship, to overcome adversity, to do good, and to protect those who are more vulnerable than ourselves.
    That inner strength has the power to heal and to change the world for the better. To me, true strength lies not in physical power, but in the human spirit.
}

## A message to the future

@Interviewer {
    Do you think today’s young generation understands the scale of the tragedy of the Semipalatinsk Nuclear Test Site?
}

@Karipbek {
    Helping young people understand and remember this history is our greatest mission. That is why we, the veterans of the Nevada-Semipalatinsk movement, continue speaking about this tragedy without growing tired.
    I clearly remember the early days of our movement. We established school and university chapters throughout Kazakhstan, encouraging young people to join our campaign. Students helped us collect signatures calling for the closure of the test site.
    I remember walking into one room and seeing enormous stacks of paper filled with thousands of signatures. I asked in amazement, “How did you collect so many in such a short time?” Olzhas Suleimenov smiled proudly and replied: “Karipbek, our young people did all of this.”
    Today’s generation has a responsibility to know this history. They are the ones who will inherit this land, and it is they who will decide whether their own children grow up in a peaceful world, or one overshadowed by fear.
}

@Interviewer {
    Why is it still important to continue telling these stories today?
}

@Karipbek {
    Because our ultimate goal is the complete elimination of nuclear weapons from our planet.
    As long as greed and the pursuit of power continue to shape the world, achieving that goal will not be easy. But we have no choice except to keep trying. As the saying goes, constant dripping wears away the stone.
    If we remain silent, the problem will not disappear. That is why we must continue speaking about it loudly, persistently, and at every level of society.
    We need stronger international agreements. Why shouldn’t the international community adopt a universal law that completely prohibits the development, sale, purchase, transfer, or distribution of nuclear weapons and their components? Such actions should be recognized as crimes against humanity.
    I sincerely believe that making this vision a reality is the responsibility of a new generation of thoughtful, compassionate young people. The future of our planet is quite literally in their hands.
}

@Interviewer {
    When someone reads this interview many years from now, what message would you like to leave for future generations?
}

@Karipbek {
    My message is very simple.
    I hope your world will have fewer children’s tears and many more joyful smiles.
    I hope your generation creates a world that is cleaner, kinder, and better than the one we have today. I hope humanity chooses to invest its greatest resources and brightest minds not in building weapons of destruction, but in developing cures for diseases, advancing technology, and exploring space for peaceful purposes.
    Above all, I hope future generations never lose their compassion.
    Protect nature. Be kind to animals. I dream of a future in which humanity truly learns to care for the creatures that share this planet with us, because they are just as vulnerable before us as children are.
    Live in peace. Create rather than destroy. And always remember that the greatest value on Earth is life itself.
}
