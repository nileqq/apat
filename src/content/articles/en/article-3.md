Today's article features Ilya. The story Ilya shared with us today has survived in his family only in fragments. Many details about his great-grandfather's fate were lost over time, so today it is more a collection of memories passed down through generations than a complete account.

However, even these details are enough to reveal the tragedy of a man who was devoted to the state, worked honestly, raised a large family, and yet ended up a victim of repressions. The fate of Sergey Izmaylovich Kudryavtsev serves as a reminder that during the years of repression, neither merit, nor loyalty, nor a family's contribution to building the Soviet state could protect anyone from lawlessness.

Political repressions in Kazakhstan from the 1920s to the 1950s involved the mass destruction of the intelligentsia, dekulakization, the organization of an artificial famine (which claimed around 1.5 million lives), and the forced deportation of entire peoples. Millions of people became victims of the totalitarian regime, and the total number of convicted individuals in the Kazakh ASSR exceeded 100,000.

The repressions affected the leaders of the <span class="term" title="Alash Orda was the first Kazakh national autonomous government, formed by Kazakh intellectuals in 1917. It was dissolved by Soviet authorities in 1920, and its leaders were later subjected to repression.">Alash Orda</span> party (Alikhan Bukeikhanov, Akhmet Baitursynov, Mirjaqip Dulatov), as well as scientists, writers, and public figures.

These repressions can be viewed not only as the persecution of people striving to create an independent Kazakh state, but also as the destruction of Kazakhstan's intellectual and cultural heritage. The Kazakh intelligentsia (the most educated segment of society at the time) sought to develop their people through education, preserving the native language, enlightenment, and reforms. However, the Stalinist regime subjected representatives of this intellectual elite to mass repressions, torture, and executions, depriving society of leaders who could have offered an alternative path of development. In doing so, Soviet authorities eliminated potential centers of resistance and solidified their control over the region.

Kazakhstan was also home to some of the largest labor camps: ALZHIR (Akmola Camp for Wives of Traitors to the Motherland), Karlag (Karaganda Labor Camp), and Steplag, where prisoners were exiled from all over the USSR.

## The Great-Grandfather's Fate

@Interviewer {
    Your great-grandfather was repressed by Soviet authorities. What was he like before his arrest?
}

@Ilya {
    My great-grandfather, Sergey Izmaylovich Kudryavtsev, was born in 1905. According to my grandmother's memories, he was a strict but very responsible and hardworking man. He was also quite talented: he taught himself to play the guitar, mandolin, balalaika, and piano.
    Before his arrest, my great-grandfather worked as an engineer, was an inventor-innovator, and served as the director of the city water supply system. He was raising five children, enjoyed the respect of those around him, and was sincerely loyal to the Soviet government.
    A family story illustrates how much he was valued: when my grandmother got a job at the Kirov Plant, the enterprise director, Sutyushev (I believe his name was Midkhat Usmanovich), insisted on being invited to her future wedding because he wanted to sit at the same table as her father.
}

@Interviewer {
    What happened to your great-grandfather?
}

@Ilya {
    Despite his loyalty to the system, my great-grandfather was caught in the gears of the state machine. He was sent to a camp in the Almaty region.
}

> "During the years of Stalinist repressions, there were no permanent corrective labor camps (GULAG) in the Almaty region; however, it housed the main transit/distribution hub and execution grounds. The most notorious site is the Zhanalyk area (village) in the Talgar district, where executed prisoners were secretly buried."

@Ilya {
    According to my grandmother, he returned from there missing an eye and with crippled legs.
    During the investigation, he was forced to sign a confession stating he was an "enemy of the people," but he refused. He survived by a miracle. Later, he was rehabilitated. As the family story goes, this was helped by someone in the party leadership who knew him personally. In the end, my great-grandfather lived to be 69 years old.
    The most painful part of this story is that his father fought on the side of the Reds in Chapayev's division, essentially helping build the very system that later turned against his own family.
}

> "Vasily Ivanovich Chapayev was one of the most famous military figures of the Russian Civil War (1917-1922) and a commander in the Red Army."

@Interviewer {
    How did your great-grandfather's family live during his arrest?
}

@Ilya {
    Unfortunately, very little information about that period has survived in our family. My grandmother was born in 1945, so she did not witness those events herself and only recounted what she heard from her elders.
    I only recall one family story: when my great-grandfather returned from the camp, his mother supposedly died right at the table from overwhelming joy. I don't know how accurate this is, but that is how the story was passed down in our family. The family was not deprived of their housing and was not evicted.
}

@Interviewer {
    What inventions or rationalization proposals belonged to your great-grandfather?
}

@Ilya {
    As far as I know, inventions in the field of water supply networks and improvements to water standpipes in the Kazakh ASSR belonged to my great-grandfather.
}

## A Lesson from History

@Interviewer {
    Why did his father's service in Vasily Chapayev's division fail to protect the family?
}

@Ilya {
    That remains an open question. I can only speculate, as we have no objective data left.
    Perhaps it was due to internal power struggles or someone's personal interests. For example, a desire to take a specific job position or eliminate a rival. But one cannot rule out the general atmosphere of that era, where suspicion and accusations could arise over virtually anything.
    My great-grandfather's story shows that even past services, loyalty to the system, and a family's role in its establishment could not always protect a person from the repressive machine.
}

@Interviewer {
    What message would you like to share with the younger generation of Kazakhstan?
}

@Ilya {
    It is vital for our country and society to continue reflecting on the past and decolonizing our historical memory. This year marks 35 years of Kazakhstan's independence, yet there are still people who attempt to justify the crimes of the communist regime – remembering only its achievements while forgetting the price paid by millions.
    It is essential to remember how many lives this system claimed, how much suffering it brought to the Kazakh people and other ethnic groups living in Kazakhstan, and the tragedies that took place on our land.
    The Asharshylyq (Famine), <span class="term" title="Goloshchekin's &quot;Little October&quot; was a policy of forced collectivization and sedentarization of nomadic Kazakhs, carried out in the late 1920s by Filipp Goloshchekin, First Secretary of the Kazakh Regional Party Committee. This policy was the principal cause of the 1930-1933 famine.">Goloshchekin's "Little October,"</span> mass repressions, Russification policies, environmental disasters like the drying of the Aral Sea and nuclear tests at the Semipalatinsk Test Site, the events of <span class="term" title="The events of Jeltoqsan were mass protests in Almaty on 16-19 December 1986 against a Moscow-appointed republican leader. They were the first large-scale uprising against central Soviet authority in the USSR's history.">Jeltoqsan</span>, and the deportations of Chechens, Germans, Koreans, Balts, and other peoples – all of this must be known and spoken about openly.
    Citizens must understand who made the decisions that led to these tragedies, who resisted injustice, and at what cost Kazakhstan achieved its independence.
    The phrase "Never again" should not sound exclusively in the context of World War II. It must apply to all crimes against humanity and serve as a constant reminder that such tragedies must never be allowed to happen again.
}
