Today's character of Qazaq Mura journal is Meruert. In this interview, Meruert shares the remarkable story of her family's survival during the Kazakh Famine of the 1930s and their subsequent migration to Xinjiang, China.

Through memories passed down by Meruert's maternal grandparents, she recounts their life in exile, their decision to return to Kazakhstan, and the sacrifices they made while crossing the Soviet border. The interview also explores the lasting impact of political repression on her family's identity, including the loss of her paternal grandmother's original surname and the significance of the family's double surname.

In Kazakhstan, triggered by Stalin's collectivization policies, the famine claimed the lives of an estimated 1.5-2.3 million people, with approximately 1.3-1.5 million ethnic Kazakhs perishing. More than 600,000 Kazakhs fled their homeland in search of survival, many crossing into neighboring China.

Beyond documenting one family's journey, the interview reflects the experiences of thousands of Kazakh families who endured famine, displacement, and political persecution. Meruert discusses how these stories shaped her understanding of history, identity, and resilience, emphasizing the importance of preserving oral histories so that future generations can learn not only historical facts, but also the human experiences behind them.

## About Herself

@Interviewer {
    Meruert, can you briefly introduce yourself?
}

@Meruert {
    I am 35 years old. My family comes from the Almaty Region in southern Kazakhstan, although today I live in the eastern part of the country. I work as a psychologist and am currently pursuing a master's degree at Utrecht University in the Netherlands.
}

## Fleeing to China

@Interviewer {
    Why and when did your family decide to flee to China?
}

@Meruert {
    It all began with the collectivization campaign in the mid 1930s, when the Soviet government confiscated the belongings and livestock of Kazakh families. To escape these policies, my great-grandparents decided to flee to China. My maternal grandmother was about 12 years old at the time. They settled in <span class="term" title="Kulja (present-day Yining) is a city on the banks of the Ili River and the administrative center of the Ili Kazakh Autonomous Prefecture in Xinjiang.">Kulja</span>, in China's <span class="term" title="Xinjiang is an autonomous region in northwestern China bordering Kazakhstan. Many Kazakhs fleeing the famine of the 1930s settled here.">Xinjiang</span> region.
}

@Interviewer {
    How did your family make the journey from Kazakhstan to China?
}

@Meruert {
    My family was not alone. They were part of a much larger group of Kazakh families fleeing together.
    Some people travelled on horseback, while others walked for long distances because there were not enough horses for everyone. Many of them were children.
    They crossed the <span class="term" title="The Ili River rises in China's Xinjiang region and flows through Kazakhstan's Almaty region to Lake Balkhash. It served as the main border-crossing point both for those fleeing the famine and for those who later returned.">Ili River</span> and travelled through the mountains before finally reaching Xinjiang.
    The journey was exhausting. For much of the way, they walked barefoot. Whenever someone became too tired to continue, they would ride on a horse or in a cart for a short distance before continuing on foot.
    It was an incredibly difficult journey, but they had no other choice.
}

@Interviewer {
    What were the first years of life in China like?
}

@Meruert {
    My grandmother always remembered those years warmly. The local people welcomed them with kindness and generosity.
    On my mother's side of the family, my grandparents lived among Uyghur and Nogai communities. They often spoke about how friendly and supportive their neighbors were. At first, they rented a small house and slowly rebuilt their lives.
    My grandmother learned many practical skills from the women around her, including sewing, knitting, cooking, and various traditional beauty practices. She often said that despite arriving as refugees with almost nothing, they were treated with dignity and compassion by the people who welcomed them. Those memories stayed with her for the rest of her life.
    In Xinjiang, my grandmother met my grandfather. They married when she was 15 and he was 25 years old. While living in China, they had six children.
}

## Returning to Kazakhstan

@Interviewer {
    You mentioned that your family moved back from China to Kazakhstan. What happened?
}

@Meruert {
    Several years later, when my grandparents' children (my uncles and aunts) were around 10 or 12 years old, the political situation began to change.
    My grandparents felt increasing pressure from the Chinese communist authorities, who began introducing policies that limited family size. Concerned about their future and the well-being of their six children, they eventually decided to return to Kazakhstan.
}

@Interviewer {
    How did your grandparents cope with leaving their home for a second time? Were they hopeful, or was it a painful experience?
}

@Meruert {
    For my grandmother, it was heartbreaking.
    She loved living in Kulja. She had built a life there, surrounded by neighbors and a community she cared about deeply. Leaving that home was extremely difficult for her.
    My grandfather, however, believed returning to Kazakhstan was the right decision. As the head of the family, he took responsibility for making that choice. My grandmother trusted him completely and believed he would protect the family.
}

@Interviewer {
    How did they return?
}

@Meruert {
    They crossed the border through the Ili River.
    In China, my grandfather worked as a rickshaw driver. He was very athletic and earned a living transporting passengers in a hand-pulled rickshaw, which allowed him to save a considerable amount of money.
    Leaving China itself was not difficult. However, when they reached the Soviet border, everything changed. Soviet officials demanded documents proving ownership of all their belongings. My grandmother had gold and silver jewelry, but my grandfather understood that they would most likely lose everything. Rather than surrender their possessions, he threw them into the Ili River.
    They entered Kazakhstan carrying only two belongings: a copper samovar and a traditional cup made from animal bone.
}

@Interviewer {
    What happened after they arrived in Kazakhstan?
}

@Meruert {
    Unfortunately, when they arrived, they could not find any of their relatives.
    Without a place to live, they travelled to Kyrgyzstan, where a local family welcomed them into their home. They later rented a small clay house and, with almost no money left, earned income by renting out their copper samovar to other people. Despite living in extremely modest conditions, my grandparents raised all six of their children there.
    In the 1950s, they finally returned to Kazakhstan. The Soviet authorities issued them documents and provided them with a house. My grandfather worked as a security guard, while my grandmother also found work as a night security guard at a factory.
    After returning to Kazakhstan, they had several more children, including my mother and one of my uncles. Sadly, not all of the children survived. In total, my grandmother gave birth to fourteen children, but only nine lived to adulthood.
}

## The Copper Samovar

@Interviewer {
    What does the copper samovar symbolize for your family today?
}

@Meruert {
    The copper samovar became a symbol of survival and resilience.
    When my grandparents arrived with almost nothing, they exchanged it as payment for the small clay building where they were allowed to live. That simple object provided shelter for the entire family.
    Whenever I think about the samovar, I imagine my grandparents living in a tiny room with six children, trying to build a new life from nothing.
}

## A Hidden History

@Interviewer {
    You mentioned that there was another hidden story connected to your paternal grandmother's surname. Could you tell us about it?
}

@Meruert {
    My paternal grandparents also fled to China during the famine. I do not know many details about my father's grandparents, but I know much more about my grandmother's story because, before I got married, I carried both her surname and my grandfather's surname.
    As a child, I always wondered why most people had only one surname while I had two.
    When my grandmother was a little girl, her parents were respected members of their community. From what I understand, they served as local judges or community leaders. During the political repressions, they became targets of the Soviet authorities and were considered politically unreliable.
    Fearing for their children's lives, the family made a difficult decision to save them. My grandmother was entrusted to another Kazakh family from the same tribe. When they fled across the border into China, they gave her a different surname to conceal her identity and protect her from persecution. That is how she became Sapiyeva.
    To this day, we do not know what her original surname was. That part of our family history has been lost.
    Years later, in China, she met my paternal grandfather, and they married. To honor the family that had saved my grandmother's life, my grandfather gave all three of his sons a double surname. It was his way of expressing gratitude and ensuring that the memory of both families would continue. Today, only my father and my two uncles still carry those double surnames.
}

## A Legacy for Future Generations

@Interviewer {
    What emotions do you feel when you think about your grandparents' experiences?
}

@Meruert {
    Above all, gratitude.
    Sometimes it is difficult to imagine everything they endured. They survived famine, crossed mountains and rivers as children, became refugees, built new lives in another country. Then they left everything behind once again and returned to Kazakhstan, where they had to start over from nothing.
    Despite all of those hardships, they never spoke with hatred. Instead, they always remembered the kindness of the people who helped them along the way. That is probably the greatest lesson they passed on to us.
}

@Interviewer {
    Do you think your family's story reflects the experience of an entire generation?
}

@Meruert {
    Absolutely.
    When I once asked my uncles why only our family had been forced to flee, they answered, "It wasn't only us."
    They told me that our grandparents saw countless other families making the same journey. They remembered parents burying children beside the road because the children were too weak to continue. They remembered families crying, children becoming separated from their parents, and people wandering in search of relatives.
    Our family's story is only one among thousands. It represents the experience of an entire generation of Kazakhs who endured the famine and were forced to leave their homeland in search of survival.
}

@Interviewer {
    When you tell this story today, what do you hope people understand?
}

@Meruert {
    I hope people understand that history is deeply personal.
    When we speak about the Kazakh Famine or political repression, we often focus on numbers. But behind every statistic was a family like mine.
    My grandparents were ordinary people trying to protect their children. They crossed mountains, rivers, and international borders simply to survive.
    For me, these events are not just history. They are my family's memories.
}

@Interviewer {
    What would you like younger generations to understand about these events?
}

@Meruert {
    Many of my nephews and nieces still do not know these stories. Some members of my family believe they are too young to understand.
    I see it differently. These stories are difficult, but they are part of who we are. They create a connection between generations.
    If we stop telling them, that connection will disappear. History deserves to be remembered. That is why projects like Qazaq Mura are so important. They preserve memories that cannot be found in textbooks.
}
